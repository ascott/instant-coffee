Light It Up 

Brigitte Lochhead, Vancouver BC
Carlos Coln, Vancouver BC
Klaus Pinter, Vienna Austria
Mathias MacPhee, Dawson City Yukon
Stuart Ward, Vancouver BC
Trevor Van den Eijnden, Vancouver BC

06 February 2016 - 19 March 2016

The artworks in Light It Up at Arnica Artist Run Centre February 5 to 
March 19, offer a direction in contemporary artistic practice that 
displays subject matter and cultural references. The works are discrete 
views into other worlds and provide a complex relationship between the 
kind of light used and the story being told. For example, Trevor van den 
Eijnden's Lawn, uses surveillance glass to multiply the tiny LED lit pink 
flamingos into a gridded suburbia that seems to extend forever. Stuart 
Ward's works are reminiscent of stained glass windows that feature a 
kaleidoscope of video views of natural phenomena. Other works: 
reflect their surroundings while they emit light (Lockheed); cradle fiber 
optic northern lights between the antlers of an actual caribou 
(MacPhee); use the playful quality of glow sticks to draw the viewer 
through dance steps (Pinter); and spell out ethnic rejection no lugar (no 
place) in the classic invitation of neon signage (Coln).

Admission to Arnica gallery is always free

Arnica Artist Run Centre
Old Courthouse Cultural Centre
7 Seymour St. West
Kamloops, B.C., Canada
V2C 1E4

Please contact Arnica's Gallery Coordinator, Christine Beaton, at
arnicaarc@gmail.com for more information.

<a href="/instant-coffee/listings/2016-02-18-02-back-gallery-project-x-files-the-truth-is-here-feb-16">02. EXHIBITION | Back Gallery Project | X- Files: The Truth is Here | FEB 16</a>

Tuesday - Saturday 1-5PM

X- Files: The Truth is Here (The Making of Season 10)
Exhibition Dates: February 16, 2016 until February 27, 2016

The X-Files is an pop culture science fiction television series created 
by Chris Carter. The program originally aired from September 10, 1993, 
to May 19, 2002, spanning nine seasons with 202 episodes and a 
feature film before returning with a second film in 2008 and a tenth 
season, six-episode miniseries in 2016. The series revolves around 
FBI special agents Fox Mulder (David Duchovny) and Dana Scully 
(Gillian Anderson) who investigate X-Files: marginalized, unsolved 
cases involving paranormal phenomena.

The first five seasons of The X-Files were filmed and produced in 
Vancouver, BC, before eventually moving to Los Angeles. The series 
later returned to Vancouver to film the 2008 film The X-Files: I Want to 
Believe, as well as the 2016 six-episode season.

The exhibition pays homage to the worldwide phenomenon that was 
pivotal in giving Vancouver the name of "Hollywood North" and 
focuses on episode 1 and 6, all the original scripts, props and objects 
that are included in this exhibition were used during the last summer's 
production in Vancouver. The material comes directly from director, 
producer and writer Chris Carter. 

The exhibition is a peek into filmmaking making and the various 
considerations and details that need to take place.

Please note that no cameras or cell phones will be allowed during the 
exhibition or until the last episode has aired on February 22, 2016.

<a href="/instant-coffee/listings/2016-02-18-03-macaulay-co-fine-art-judy-chartrand-feb-17">03. EXHIBITION | Macaulay & Co. Fine Art | Judy Chartrand | FEB 17</a>
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Macaulay & Co. Fine Art is pleased to present a series of ceramic 
sculptures, Mtis Soup, by Judy Chartrand in our gallery from February 
17 - March 26, 2016. Please note there will be no evening reception, 
but will open at noon on Wednesday, February 17th. 

I have made a series of hand-built ceramic Mtis soup cans fashioned 
after the Campbell's soup logo and design. Initially I had wanted to 
create a series of soup cans incorporating traditional native fare. I 
decided to study the graphic design on an actual soup can so I went to 
my cupboard and all I had were cans of Campbell's soup. As I 
examined a can, I noticed the red and white division of the graphics 
and was inspired to create a Mtis version utilizing the same graphics 
as the Campbell's soup brand. Although the soup is a mass marketed 
consumer item that is a recognizable symbol of the American way of 
life, First Nation's people on or off the reserve appear to be erased 
from the overall picture as active participants in this consumer society. 
This points back to the issues of visibility and invisibility. By including a 
native identity into the Campbell's soup can series, I have done a 
couple of things. First, I have made a proactive gestureindicating the 
fact that Native people also have an invested interest in the urban, 
consumer, and modern experience. Secondly, I have inserted a cultural 
identity into pop art, by referencing Andy Warhol's Campbell's soup 
series and giving them a Mtis/Cree identity. This is in reference to 
Coast Salish artist, Lawrence Paul Yuxweluptun who created a large 
painting of an "Indian" hotdog using Northwest coast design elements 
in the form of ovoids. He states: "Pop art had been internationalized. I 
culturalized it." Furthermore, I have acknowledged Mtis heritage since 
the Campbell name is a Mtis name in many Native communities. The 
half-red and half-white soup cans are symbolic representations of the 
Mtis culture. I have incorporated Mtis Cree and English text to 
describe the contents of the cans, which consist mainly of traditional 
Native fare. Even though the Campbell's soup company advertises on 
their web site that they make "regional varieties that respond to cultural 
differences," I doubt that I will ever see a soup made that is geared 
specifically to satisfy the palate of a First Nations cultural group and 
in reality, I don't think I really want to! 

- Judy Chartrand

Judy Chartrand is Manitoba Cree and was born in Kamloops, BC. She 
grew up with her thirteen siblings in Vancouver's downtown eastside. 
Her interest in art began at an early age, as she spent a lot of her time 
viewing art at the Vancouver Museum. In 1989 she began painting 
store bought ceramics and was planning to sell her work at a local 
Native Arts & Craft Fair in Vancouver. She did not have the funds to 
continue to purchase the ceramics, so she decided to make her own.
She lives and works in Vancouver.

In Conversation: Cindy Mochizuki with Hannah Jickling, Helen Reed, 
and Vanessa Kwan on Artistic Collaborations with Children I Thursday, 
Feb 18 @ 7:00 PM

What is the radical potential of artists working with children? How do 
we understand agency and authorship in projects involving young 
people? How might such projects point to broader questions around 
the ethics of engagement in contemporary art practice, and to new 
(and often destabilizing) forms of interaction within the gallery and 
beyond? How might curators or institutions shift their practices to 
support a wider range of complex, generous, and attentive play, in 
younger communities and in a wider sense? These are some of the 
questions that will propel a conversation between artists Cindy 
Mochizuki (Access Gallery), Hannah Jickling, Helen Reed, and 
Vanessa Kwan (Other Sights for Artists' Projects) on the occasion of 
their concurrent artistic collaborations with children. We view this 
informal event as a generative jumping-off point for a robust, longer-
term discussion. Children are welcome at this event, of course!

HANNAH JICKLING experiments with the possibilities of form, 
participation and meaning-making across disciplines and publics. She 
frequently collaborates with HELEN REED, whose artistic practice 
explores her interest in participatory culture, affinity groups and 
fantasy-based subcultures. VANESSA KWAN is an artist and curator 
whose current projects include Jickling and Reed's public artwork 
situated at Queen Alexandra Elementary School (produced by Other 
Sights). CINDY MOCHIZUKI is an interdisciplinary artist engaged in a 
wide range of projects that evoke history, memory, and the imaginary. 
She holds an MFA in Interdisciplinary Studies from the School For 
Contemporary Arts at Simon Fraser University.

<a href="/instant-coffee/listings/2016-02-18-08-the-bakery-and-dynamo-art-association-bend-toward-the-sun-fe">08. OPENING | The Bakery and Dynamo Art Association | Bend Toward the Sun... | FEB 19</a>

The Bakery and Dynamo Arts Association present Bend Toward the 
Sun, Bring the Sky Beneath Your Feet, a group exhibition presented 
across two spaces.

*Opening Reception(s) Friday February 19th, 2016

The Bakery 6pm-8pm, 1670 Franklin Street, Vancouver (upstairs)

Dynamo Arts Association 8pm-10pm, Suite 103 - 30 East 6th Ave, 
Vancouver

Johnny Burgess (Vancouver), Jessica Groome (Berlin), Steve & 
Meghann Hubert (Vancouver),Jenine Marsh(Toronto), Ella Dawn 
McGeough (Toronto) and Les Ramsay (Vancouver). Curated by 
Jasmine Reimer (Toronto).

Bend Toward the Sun, Bring the Sky Beneath Your Feet renders the 
words 'flexible' and 'material' as indefinite. Material, the matter from 
which all things are made or can be made is dilated, including not only 
physical objects and images but also mind and spirit. Materials produce 
materials. Seeped in charm and grace, flexibility is slippery evading 
specificity and explanation.

Unexpectedly, we are confronted with the danger in bending, 
stretching, in risking a break, in the pleasure of meandering. Splinters 
and sagging slits, universal footwear and monochrome flora, wayward 
glue and terry cloth comfort set up the circumstances in which flexibility 
is equally an advantage and handicap. Confronting the ostensible 
relationship between supple maker and elastic thing, this exhibition 
questions the purpose of limitations; revised and confronted, 
boundaries become fluid, distant and permeable.

*Exhibition runs February 19th-March 4th, 2016.

Panel Discussion: Responding to Graeme Patterson: Secret Citadel
Surrey Art Gallery
Saturday February 20, 34:30pm

Join author Lee Henderson, multimedia artist Marina Roy, and writer 
Dr. Jerry Zaslove as they explore major themes in the exhibit Graeme 
Patterson: Secret Citadel, including memory, dream, storytelling, 
animal-human relations, boyhood, friendship, and the use of animation 
in contemporary art. Refreshments will be available for the duration of 
this informal event. Admission is free.

Following their individual presentations, the panelists will enter into a 
conversation with each other and the audience, facilitated by Surrey Art 
Gallery Curator of Exhibitions and Collections Jordan Strom.

About the Presenters:

Lee Henderson is an award-winning author and contributing editor to 
the arts magazines Border Crossings in Canada and Contemporary in 
the UK. His first novel, The Man Game, won the 2009 Ethel Wilson 
Fiction Prize and 2009 City of Vancouver Book Award. He teaches 
Creative Writing at the University of Victoria.

Marina Roy is a Vancouver-based artist working across a variety of 
media, including painting, drawing, sculpture, animation, video, 
performance, and writing. She has exhibited work across Canada, as 
well as in Europe and the United States. Roy teaches Visual Arts at the 
University of British Columbia.

Jerry Zaslove is a scholar who has has taught at Simon Fraser 
University in Literature, Humanities, and the Social History of Art, 
influenced but not limited by the traditions of the relationship of radical 
thinking to the arts, psychoanalysis, and anarchism. He is the Founding 
Director of the Institute for the Humanities and has published numerous 
essays and monographs on the subjects he loves and teaches.

About Graeme Patterson: Secret Citadel
Secret Citadel is a narrative exhibition that uses large and miniature-
scale sculptures and stop-motion film and video to tell a story of 
friendship through four large-scale sculptural installations containing 
detailed miniature worlds and complemented by stop-motion animation. 
An anthropomorphized bison and cougar represent two boys in this 
visual coming-of-age tale that is as playful as it is poignant. Although 
based on specific memories of the artist's past, Secret Citadel draws 
you into its captivating worlds by highlighting universal themes of love 
and loss, play and competition, companionship and loneliness. Co-
curated by Melissa Bennett and Sarah Fillmore, Secret Citadel is on 
tour from the Art Gallery of Hamilton and Art Gallery of Nova Scotia. 
The Surrey Art Gallery is the only contemporary art museum in British 
Columbia to present this exhibition.

Surrey Art Gallery gratefully acknowledges the financial assistance of 
the City of Surrey, BC Arts Council and the province of British 
Columbia, the Canada Council for the Arts, and the Surrey Art Gallery 
Association.

Surrey Art Gallery is located in the Surrey Arts Centre, on 88 Avenue, 1 
block east of King George Blvd, in Bear Creek Park. Phone: 604-501-
5566 or www.surrey.ca/arts

A Short Story
Al Freeman, Chris Hood, Monique Mouton, Adrianne Rubenstein
Saturday, February 27th Saturday, March 26th 
Opening Saturday, February 27th 6:00 10:00PM
17 West Broadway, Vancouver
RSVP

I wanted to get started writing, and my friend suggested I begin with a 
short story: a character study with one main event, and an abundance 
of shadows (in a short story, absence takes on a presence). There is 
drama in a short story, and events are noted with room for a few stray 
suggestions. There is a crux, a clang, as one thing bangs against 
another. In a short story, there is one distinct mark traced over the 
earth.

If you write a number of short stories you can work your way up to a big 
one, it's like an exercise. Characters can take shape, like seeds newly 
planted, the beginning of a life. The short story has momentum; it is 
also just long enough without getting you too entangled. It is not The 
Odyssey.

Another reason for a short story is how perfectly it describes the space 
around one of Monique Mouton's breathlessly charged forms. 
Shhhh... they say, as they pull you under the water. Ms. Mouton grew 
up in Colorado and earned her Bachelor's degree at Emily Carr in 
Vancouver. She is familiar with mountains and the wispy things that 
grow on them. Here in inclement weather, behind a boulder, is life that 
seems to exist only by virtue of the sun's pulling it out of its hiding. 
Slow erosion, a dominant whisper. Distinct in color and in softness, her 
paintings yearn beautifully and strangely, describing a state of being 
that is bittersweet until it pops-- and becomes pure and transcendent.

Al Freeman's work declares an identity that is comedic, sincere and 
baffling. Her muse, a man named Ortelio, a former flame and 
concubine, an everyman who worked in I.T. on Wall Street, wears 
many costumes and poses here. You feel for him because he is a 
sympathetic character, not unlike a bashful elephant dressed in a tutu. 
The portrayal is as much self-portrait as commentary on gender bias in 
art. Al stands for Allison, and the artist works with her left hand, though 
she is predisposed to use her right; both stances invert conventions 
instituted during Ms. Freeman's matriculation at the number one art 
school in America. These may be clues that Ms. Freeman does not 
want you to know, but here they are laid out on the table for the sake of 
the short story.

People think they know things, but stories are vastly distorted when 
they come in the form of memory rather than document (and even 
history is distorted, as we all know). An early work of Chris Hood's 
when he was still in art school in San Francisco depicted a sticker from 
Toy Story-- a flying pig puffed out like a balloon floating over cream-
colored stationery-- with the words "Fuck Face" emblazoned over the 
top in red bubble letters. It conjures up what you think long after you 
first fall in love and you want to write the person's name in blood, the 
moment when instead you say goodbye... So Long, Fuck Face was an 
early venture into appropriation for Mr. Hood, whose endearing works 
poke at you like acupuncture needles to the heart. 

Adrianne Rubenstein makes paintings whose starting points are 
oblique memories of adolescent self-righteousness and fluttering 
identity. Images start out with a craving and are rendered ambiguously, 
hopelessly. Layers of paint and scuffling brushwork recall the swirling 
pattern from an old friend's duvet cover, an earring she coveted from a 
downtown kiosk in the 90s located on eBay in 2015, or simply a 
window at night, with a branch. The images aim to replicate the aches 
of tweenhood that go unarticulated until decades later when the smell 
of an old closet makes it all flood back into the chest. Together the 
works excavate the home she holds in her mind's eye - transformed by 
dreams and envisioned anew.

CSA Space presents
recto-verso
Lyndl Hall

Opening reception Thursday 3 March 2016, 6-9pm
3 March 2016 through 3 April 2016

Curated by Steffanie Ling

To play a game of hopscotch one marks off a series of numbered 
squares on the ground with chalk, the player then throws a small object
a pebble, a penny, a bottle capand jumps through the squares 
sequentially, avoiding the occupied square. A scotch is "an incised line 
or scratch".

Sundials are the oldest known devices for measuring time. They need 
only consist of a flat plane and a "gnomon" that will throw a shadow 
onto the surface. Lines marked on the surface will indicate the time of 
day as the shadow passes over them. The angle of the gnomon 
corresponds to the latitude degree of the site used.

Chapbooks are an early form of popular literature that was
prevalent in the 17th century and coincided with advances in the 
technology of the printing press and the rise of literacy in the lower 
classes. Cheap to make and cheap to buy, these small, paper covered 
booklets were printed on a single sheet of paper that was folded down 
in halves. A sheet folded in sextodecimo (also 16mo or 16) is folded in 
half 4 times to make 16 leaves. Also called a 32-page signature.

With these ideas as a starting point, the works in recto-verso are 
concerned with division and allocation in an undifferentiated planethe 
interpretation of omens and signsas well as measuring, counting, 
rhyming, and daydreams.
