Clip/Stamp/Fold 6: Response Talk

Peter Miller

Thursday, November 6, 2008, 7pm

In conjunction with Clip/Stamp/Fold 6, the Contemporary Art Gallery has
invited architects, writers and publishers from the region to respond to
the exhibition. Peter Miller is the third and last in a series of these
respondents.

Peter Miller is the founder of Peter Miller Architectural & Design Books in
Seattle, a specialized book store that has been in operation for thirty
years. After earning an Education degree from Harvard Graduate School he
moved west, establishing Montana Books and Montana Publishing Company in
1975. After publishing a variety of titles including Aldous Huxley's The
Art of Seeing, he went on to specialize in architectural and design books.
With a background as an educator focused on the architectural and design
field, Peter Miller has had a far reaching influence on a generation of
artists, architects and designers.

Clip/Stamp/Fold: The Radical Architecture of Little Magazines 196X-197X, is
curated by internationally renowned architectural theorist Beatriz Colomina
and a group of PhD students at Princeton University, School of
Architecture. The exhibition takes stock of over seventy little magazines
from the sixties and seventies. It has met great critical acclaim
especially for remaining geographically specific to each city where it is
presented. In the CAG version forty-four vintage architectural magazines
were collected from Vancouver architects and artists for the 6th
presentation of Clip/Stamp/Fold. The exhibition was first presented in New
York at the Storefront for Art and Architecture in 2006.

Clip/Stamp/Fold 6 runs until Sunday, November 9, 2008.

Arts Council, the BC Gaming Policy and Enforcement Branch and the BC Arts

<a href="/instant-coffee/listings/2008-11-06-02-grace-gallery-andy-dixon-how-to-draw-everything-nov-6">02. OPENING | grace-gallery | Andy Dixon: How To Draw Everything | NOV 6</a>

grace-gallery is elated to present

Andy Dixon
How To Draw Everything
opening thursday november 6th, 8pm
http://www.facebook.com/event.php?eid=28283464294

www.grace-gallery.com

A child has just finished a finger painting assignment in her kindergarten
art class. To the teacher's dismay, Faye's painted fingers have fashioned
together a word of extreme offense. Admittedly the four letters are not
clear as day but the teacher deems them present nonetheless. After all, it
is somewhat hard to ignore such profanity. However, Faye's intention was to
paint a horse grazing in a field beside a tree, which she maintains she has
done successfully. She is proud of her horse painting and doesn't
understand her teacher's reaction. A debate ensues amongst the school's
staff: how much offense can be affixed to the four letters in Faye's
painting if the artist's intent is absent? Is that word still present once
it has been established that it only occurred accidentally? And although
the painting looks nothing like a horse grazing in a field beside a tree,
how can anyone argue that it is in fact anything but? Does the artist have
complete veto power over audience interpretation? How To Draw Everything is
a reaction to such an idea. It plays with the dilemma of interpretation vs.
intent. Perhaps such ambiguity is all part of the undeniable beauty of art:
the audience can be touched so personally by it because viewing and
interpreting art is a personal endeavor. Perhaps a map of the artist's
intent or the over-intellectualizing of her work is stifling to the
audience, stunting its chance to reach out and evoke something hidden and
personal within the viewer.

Until We Have A Helicopter

Open to all

Exhibition runs Nov 7 to Nov 29

Gallery Atsui
602 East Hastings (at Princess)

By Appointment

In tune with our mandate to host independent curators, Gallery Atsui is
please to have our first guest curation by Wes Cameron and Matthew
Robertson of Until We Have A Helicopter.

Until We Have A Helicopter continue to work in a dimension between artist
and curator with their latest exhibition at Gallery Atsui. By inviting
artists to work with them to create an exhibition about an imagined history
of their collaborative entity, Wes Cameron and Matthew Robertson entrust
the mythology behind Until We Have A Helicopter to seven adaptable artists
familiar with their practice. Raymond Boisjoly, Mark Dudiak, Babak Golkar,
Arvo Leo, Robert Niven, Erica Stocking, and Ron Tran have worked with and
against UWHAH to produce an ad-hoc, multi-faceted narrative in a variety of
media.

About UWHAH

Wes Cameron and Matthew Robertson formed Until We Have A Helicopter in 2005
in order to have their operation of Lobby Gallery identified as a
project-based artwork by a collaborative entity. While facilitating
site-specific installations in the lobby of Vancouver's Dominion Hotel they
looked on the venture with an "if you build it, they will come" attitude.
By erecting and lighting a large white wall, they established a starting
point for artists to consider the possibilities and complications of the
unique, semi-public exhibition opportunity.

Until We Have A Helicopter continues to plan, and realize by invitation,
site-specific projects in a variety of settings, both public and private,
indoor and outdoor.

Pushing up Daisies - Scott Moore and Rob Nicholls
Opening Nov 7th 7pm to 11pm
Show runs Nov 8th to Nov 29th

"Pushing Up Daisies," a painting show featuring new works from Rob Nicholls
and Scott Moore, serves as an exploration of the uncertain; an invitation
to revisit imagined spaces of the past and to trespass through
temperamental sites of the now.

Moore's work is comprised of a vibrant, hyper-real palette applied
assuredly in a fashion that capitalizes on reductive strokes and layered
transparencies defining his subject of memorized instances in landscape.
This reductive technique also functions to describe a type of editing, and
ultimately of Moore's use of memory. Nicholls, on the other hand takes a
more definite, more violent approach to his subject; there is a sense of
danger which radiates from his compacted spaces. By using a palette which
mimics colours of rust and other products of chemical decay, combined with
the language of abstraction, Nicholls expresses a desire to breakdown and
then reassemble otherwise forgotten environments.

------------------------------------------------------------------------
Persistence: An Archive of Feminist Practices in Vancouver

November 8th, 2008 to January 31st, 2009
Opening 2-5pm Saturday November 8, 2008
Organized by Artspeak (with Jessie Caryl, Crista Dahl, Jennifer Fisher,
Elizabeth MacKenzie and Marina Roy)

Images of VIVO's archive for Persistence: An Archive of Feminist Practices
in Vancouver, 2008. photo: Ginger Scott.

Persistence: An Archive of Feminist Practices in Vancouver is part of a
citywide dialogue with the exhibition WACK! Art and the Feminist
Revolution. WACK!, at the Vancouver Art Gallery, explores the formation,
development and impact of feminism in post-war international contemporary
art from 1965 to 1980. Artspeak is presenting a collection of printed
material that offers a local perspective on feminist art production from
the 1970s to the early 90s.

Artspeak will exhibit ephemera and texts that span the creative
experimentation, political action and critical inquiry of a wide array of
feminist artists and arts collectives working in Vancouver. Including
production catalogues, screening guides, event posters, announcements, as
well as other documents, this collection of material allows for an
examination of the dynamic contribution local practices have made to both
the feminist project and creative culture at large.

Persistence: An Archive of Feminist Practices in Vancouver has been
organized to compliment VIVO Media Arts Centre's parallel screening project
November 7-8 Persistent Resistence: Early Feminist Video in Vancouver and
takes its materials largely from VIVO's archives. See www.vivomediaarts.com
for more information.

Louise Noguchi

Solo Exhibition
November 15 - December 20, 2008

Curator: Makiko Hara

Opening: Friday, November14, 8pm

at Vancouver Art Gallery, 750 Hornby Street

Publication: 12 pages color, Texts by Makiko Hara & Kerri Sakamoto

Centre A presents Toronto based Japanese-Canadian artist, Louise Noguchi,
in conjunction with WACK! Art and the Feminist Revolution at the
Vancouver Art Gallery. This first solo exhibition of Louise Noguchi in
Vancouver features two major works from her production of the last decade,
including a multi-video installation work entitled The Language of the
Rope, 1999, and her new installation works, Mirror, and Shanghai Dragon
(Moisture Forms), 2008. The Language of the Rope explores her interest in
wild-west performance skills. In the video installation, slow motion sounds
of knives swirling in mid-air and landing with a loud "thud" surround the
viewer. Visually, a number of video screens document different angles of a
traditional knife throwing performance, in which the artist, performing in
the role of the knife thrower's assistant, is attached to a revolving
target. In the new works, Mirror, and Shanghai Dragon, Noguchi examines the
role of the heroic landscape in Hollywood action films. She is interested
in the way these landscapes contribute to the narrative of the heroic
figure. The artist reconstructs artificial landscapes from popular films
such as Superman, Star Wars, Forbidden Planet, Enter the Dragon, the Lady
from Shanghai, using materials different from the original in order to give
the work a new context. Photographs representing the reflection of these
objects in a mirror will prompt viewers to question their own location
within the work. This is reminiscent of the cinematic experience in which
the viewer is an unseen participant in a scene. Overall, the exhibition
presents Noguchi's continuous exploration of social, physical and
psychological boundaries

The Morris and Helen Belkin Art Gallery is pleased to present a solo
exhibition of work by the Belgian artist David Claerbout. The exhibition
has transformed the gallery to show a selection of 8 video installations
that date from 1996 to the present.
David Claerbout draws on the conventions of film, photography, and digital
media, combining traditional technologies in the production of his works.
His works defy the expectations of the viewer, as he manipulates still
photographs to introduce movement, subtly accelerates or decelerates his
films, edits separate recordings together, and often employs narratives
that become a secondary feature to the work. What is central to the work is
the nature of time. Claerbout frequently introduces natural elements such
as sunlight and darkness as anchor points for the perception of time.
The exhibition includes "Bordeaux Piece" (2004), which is nearly 14 hours
in length, and comprised of 70 short films shot at 10 minute intervals
between 5:30 am and 10:00 pm over the course of several days. Actors play
out the same scripted scene over and over, while the slow movement of
daylight across the set becomes the organizing principle in the work, and
as Claerbout explains, "gives form to duration by means of natural light."

David Claerbout (b. 1969 Kortrjik, Belgium) lives and works in Antwerp.
Since the late 1990s, his work has been exhibited and included in public
collections such as Sammlung Goetz, Museum fur Moderne Kunst, Frankfurt, De
Pont Foundation, Boijmans van Beuningen, Hamburger Bahnhof, Centre Georges
Pompidou Musee National d'Art Moderne.
