Elizabeth McIntosh is widely regarded as one the most important abstract
painters practicing in Canada today. While her paintings are located in the
realm of abstraction, the careful organization of forms sometimes alludes
to elements of the real world. Despite the apparent simplicity of
McIntoshâ��s formal language, there is often a textural richness arising from
her artistic process, which involves the application of multiple layers of
paint, allowing for trial and error and leaving a visible reworking.

Although she is primarily known for her painting, McIntosh also works in
collage. In Violets Hair, McIntoshs solo exhibition at the Contemporary
Art Gallery, she will work in both mediums. As well as exhibiting new
paintings, she builds two large-format collages: one will wrap the exterior
of the gallery, using the window vitrines, and the other will occupy one of
the galleries; each carrying a dimensional quality. This is not the first
time McIntosh has worked with collage in this scale, but it is the first
instance in which she has built a structure as the ground.

Catriona Jeffries is very pleased to announce the forthcoming exhibition of
a major new sculptural installation by Brian Jungen, which will take form
over a two month progression of exhibition making. Jungen's sculptural
practice which shapeshifts materials claimed as both commercial products
and natural resources will be enacted within a provisional workshop within
the gallery, functioning as a constantly shifting site of production. As a
result of this process a number of works will be completed and in turn
installed in the gallery; Jungen will work towards particular moments of
production at which time the gallery will be closed for two installation
periods: November 30 - December 5 2010 and again from December 20 -
January 3 2011.

Brian Jungen (1970) was born in Fort St. John, British Columbia and lives

<a href="/instant-coffee/listings/2010-11-18-05-helen-pitt-gallery-reoccurrence-serial-motifs-nov-19">05. Helen Pitt Gallery | Reoccurrence: Serial Motifs | NOV 19</a>
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Reoccurrence: Serial Motifs | Rebecca Brewer, Chrystal Fisher and Allison
Tweedie
Curated by Emmy Lee and Stephanie Rebick
Presented by the Helen Pitt Gallery at 221A Artist Run Centre, 221 E.
Georgia St., Vancouver BC

November 19 - December 18, 2010
Wednesday - Saturday, noon - 5pm
Opening reception and book launch, Friday, November 19, 8pm

[Images, left to right: Rebecca Brewer - The Peasant, 2010; Chrystal Fisher
- Little Mountain, 2007; Allison Tweedie - Median Lean-To, 2010]

This exhibition brings together three emerging, Vancouver-based artists who
use pattern in their work as an optical device to interrupt their
representational landscapes. These artists use abstracted repetitive forms
to organize the pictorial space, define depth, foreground-background
relations and create movement in their works. Their use of pattern
encourages the viewer to try and form optical associations to organize and
understand the visual field.

Multi-disciplinary artist Rebecca Brewer uses patterns and found imagery
from 1970s nature-based, fashion and educational magazines to create
biomorphic shapes and experiment with colour and form. Brewer layers
incised shapes to build totemic figures; the disparate patterns she employs
creates a dissonant effect for the viewer who must work to decipher an
overall image.

Chrystal Fisher's work features architectural structures that she uses to
layer the visual field and construct focal points for the viewer. In her
more abstract compositions, she uses geodesic forms to frame repetitive
motifs and layers them to create complex spatial relations. In more
representational works, she borrows directly from interior design, using
patterns found on tiling, couches and other domestic objects. While these
shapes act as quasi-architectural structures, they also reference the
history of abstract painting with the exploration of pure form.

Allison Tweedie's abstracted watercolours include figures in landscapes and
leisure scenes; she uses the fluid nature of the medium to create dreamy
scenes in which the people and their surroundings appear to blend together.
Tweedie uses oil pastel to disrupt the otherwise harmonious surface of her
images and introduce patterning which adds complexity in both its texture
and bold palette. The patterning in her work results from layering two
conflicting media that maintain their separate identities.

Although pattern is often relegated to the world of design, these artists
use repetitive motifs to challenge the visual purity of their imagery by
breaking up the picture plane. Pattern is used both as a means to insert
abstraction into representational scene as well as a building block by
which to create figuration. Brewer, Fisher and Tweedie use abstracted
repetitive motifs as a device through which to challenge the traditional
divide between representation and abstraction.

The exhibition is accompanied by a publication, Reoccurrence, containing
colour plates, an essay by the curators and a challenging dialogue between
the artists.

Since its inception as a student-run gallery in 1975, the Helen Pitt
Gallery has been through many changes. Originally named after the Vernon,
B.C. art patron who established a scholarship endowment for young artists,
the gallery survived the loss of the Vancouver School of Art's support in
the early 1980s, when that school changed its name to Emily Carr College of
Art and Design and moved to Granville Island. The Unit 306 Society For The
Democratization Of Art took over the management of the gallery in 1982,
which then became the Unit/Pitt Gallery. A subsequent move to larger
premises on Powell Street in Gastown coincided with another change of name,
this time to the ironically overstuffed Pitt International Galleries (PIG).
Two moves later, in a storefront on the advancing edge of Yaletown
gentrification, the Helen Pitt name was restored. In the fall of 2009,
faced with massive losses of revenue due to disastrous policy decisions
made by the B.C. government, the gallery closed its most recent location on
the fringes of Gastown and currently conducts its programming in whatever
spaces become available.

The Helen Pitt Gallery is financially assisted by the Canada Council, the
B.C. Arts Council, the City of Vancouver, and 2010 Legacies Now. This
exhibition and the accompanying publication are made possible partly
through the support of the Hamber Foundation. The Helen Pitt Gallery is
grateful for the support, contributions, and unpaid labour of artists,
cultural workers and other volunteers, without whom we would not be able to
continue. Special thanks to 221A Artist Run Centre.

<a href="/instant-coffee/listings/2010-11-18-07-vivo-media-arts-centre-jason-zumpano-and-shayne-ehman-nov-19">07. VIVO Media Arts Centre | Jason Zumpano and Shayne Ehman | NOV 19</a>

Jason Zumpano's work comprises sonically sculptural pieces, multi-layered
units of musical composition. In a departure from his previous work,
Zumpano is creating music whose purpose is neither performance nor pop -
rather, it aims to communicate a singular, impressionistic moment to a
listener.

The title Cyrillic Typewriter is an acknowledgement of the idiosyncrasies
of Zumpano's compositional relationship to the components of his work: the
main instrument, a keyboard, may be familiar, but its interpretation is
decidedly foreign. The music is unwritten, the playing technique is
manipulated through multi-tracked recording to emulate a more virtuosic
performance, and the product is a tumbling, at times cinematic, expression
of tone.

Accompanied by collaborator Shayne Ehman's animations, Zumpano's musical
sculpture juggles anarchic themes and repetitive sonic statements - a
sensorial montage for short attention spans.

Shayne Ehman, currently living in Toronto, is in the habit of sketching
from life and dream; maintaining an art practice which includes drawing,
painting, writing, printmaking, bookmaking, animation, sculpture,
filmsound, and songwriting. He loves working collaboratively and has worked
on projects with many artists including Jeffro Halladay, Jason Mclean,
Terry Peircey, Marc Bell, Tony Romano, Corin Sworn, Seth Scriver, Sandy
Plotnikoff and others. His artwork explores themes of enlightenment,
miracles, the supernatural, paranormal phenomena, extraterrestrial contact,
prayer, transcendental meditiation, visualization, sparks from the
racetrack, mystery, and experience.

Jason Zumpano is a pianist living in Vancouver, BC, Canada. Apart from
piano composition he has been involved in various musical ensembles such as
Zumpano, Destroyer, Sparrow, Loscil and Attics + Cellars.

Show & Tell I : Khan Lee and Ruth Scheuing

In the Gallery

Khan Lee and Ruth Scheuing, two artists in Everything Everyday participate
in a show & tell. They will present objects that they use on a daily basis
and explain their significance and influence on the artists practice and
the exhibited work. These talks will highlight the varied approaches in art
making, diverse rituals of the everyday and the different positions
material objects take in artists practices.

Born in Seoul, Korea, Khan Lee has a background in architecture and media
arts. His experiments with materials and technologies have resulted in
performance, sculpture and media works. He often works collaboratively in
contemporary art collectives. Lee is a founding member of Inter-Mission,
and an active member of Instant Coffee. His recent projects with Instant
Coffee include Light Bar during 2010 Vancouver Olympics, and Disco Fallout
Shelter in Hamburg and Toronto in 2009. As a solo artist, he mounted the
exhibition Untitled at Centre A in Vancouver in 2009.

Ruth Scheuing is an artist, educator and writer working with textiles,
focusing on how textiles communicate through patterns, language and
mythology. Her works have been exhibited internationally. Her published
writings include The Unraveling of History: Penelope and other Stories and
a book of essays Material Matters: the Art and Culture of Contemporary
Textiles published by YYZ, Toronto in 1999. A digital project Walking the
line made with a GPS tracks can be seen on <digitalthreads.ca> a web
project organized by the Textile Museum of Canada. Her work often combines
old and new technologies including GPS technology and computerized Jacquard
looms. She recently completed an artist residency at the Surrey Art Gallery
Tech lab in 2010, entitled Silkroads. She currently teaches in the Textile
Arts Program at Capilano University. <www.ruthscheuing.com>

FREE with Gallery admission.

This talk is held in conjunction with:

Everything Everyday

October 2, 2010 to January 16, 2011

The everyday is a constant and meaningful presence. It is the fundamental
rhythm of our lives and yet, within the history of art, it has rarely been
the subject of sustained examination by artists. Only in the past fifty
years with the emergence of a strong interest in pop culture, found
objects, autobiographical narrative and blogs, has the consideration of
quotidian life become a common subject in art.

The works in the exhibition are in a diverse range of media and are drawn
primarily from the Gallerys collection and augmented with loans from
artistsâ�� and private collections. The exhibition is organized around three
themes: Everyday Actions, Everyday Objects and Everyday Encounters.
Everyday Actions includes works that consider those simple, sometimes
mindless tasks we perform regularly; works in Everyday Objects take
ordinary items as their subjects; and Everyday Encounters examines those
common interactions that occur in daily life. Despite the presupposed banal
nature of the subject matter, the artists in this exhibition manage to
provoke surprising and poetic interpretations of the everyday in their
work. Everything Everyday includes works by Arabella Campbell,
Hadley+Maxwell, LAIWAN, Mona Hatoum, Gathie Falk, Ruth Scheuing, Khan Lee,
Euan Macdonald, Aganetha Dyck, Diane Borsato, Gabriel Orozco, James Welling
and others.

Everything Everyday is organized by the Vancouver Art Gallery and curated
by Bruce Grenville, senior curator.

Land of the Songhai, Hisham Mayat, 30min, 2010, video, USA/Niger
Land of the Songhai explores the music and landscape of the Songhai, around
the Niger River in Western Niger. Zarma mock possession hoedowns, Wodaabe
trance vocal performances, Spirit possession ceremonies, Godje one string
laments, contigi string masters, comsaa griots and Sahel night markets
create a bizarre and fascinating glimpse into the arid and culturally
vibrant bend in the Niger river.

Musical Brotherhoods From The Trans-Saharan Highway, Hisham Mayat, 60min,
2005, video, USA/Morocco
Filmed predominately at the Jemaa Al Fna in Marrakesh Morocco, 'Musical
Brotherhoods of the Trans-Saharan Highway' captures an assortment of
spectacular musical dramas presented live and unfiltered on the home turf
of the world's most dynamic string and drum specialists performing and
manifesting the ecstatic truth. Ancient mystical brotherhoods have been
flourishing for centuries in and around the cities of Marrakesh and
Essaouira in Morocco where the trade caravans have gathered from their long
journeys across the Trans-Saharan Highway. This is some of the last great
street music on Earth. A must see for string aficionados looking for
inspiration as electric ouds, banjos, mandolins and the Gnawa sentir peel
flesh from bone right before your eyes!

This night will mark the debut of the Volunteer Ecstatic Orchestra, a free

Chairman Maos Propaganda on Display at the AGGV

Victoria, BC-The Peoples Republic of China and the legacy of Chairman Mao
Zedong will be examined in an innovative new exhibition at the Art Gallery
of Greater Victoria. Charged with depicting the ideal of a better life
artists created thousands of propaganda posters from the 1920s through to
the 1980s.

Communist Paradise: Chinese Propaganda Posters will feature more than 70
posters from the personal collections of AGGV Curator of Asian Arts Barry
Till and UVIC Professor of Pacific and Asian Studies Richard King. The
exhibition opens on November 26 and runs through February 6, 2011.

Till and King began their collections of propaganda posters while living in
China during the late 1970s as exchange students. Both Richard and I
collected them because they offered a good snapshot of what was happening
in China while we were studying there, said Till. The posters were
extremely cheap at the time, you could buy them for about five to ten
cents. The dorm I stayed in China had plain whitewashed walls, so I
covered my room in posters to brighten things up.

With the formation of the Peoples Republic of China in 1949 Mao Zedong and
the Communist Party used posters as the primary source of communication to
a largely illiterate audience in pre-television China. Many of the
countries most talented artists and designers were hired by the various
government and party organizations that were responsible for propaganda
posters.

The posters were used to send messages of economic prosperity to the
peasants in the countryside and the workers in the industrial sector. The
men and women depicted are shown hardy and healthy with big smiles on their
faces, happily pursuing their work for a better life, Till added.

Depictions of an unselfish life in the service of the party, the community
and the country are another theme of the posters. Peoples faces in the
posters either show happiness and friendliness towards each other or
determination and hatefulness towards U.S. and British imperialism, as well
as towards the "Four Old Evils", old ideas, old culture, old customs and
old habits, commented Till.
