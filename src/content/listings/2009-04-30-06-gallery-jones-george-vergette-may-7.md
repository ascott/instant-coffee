GEORGE VERGETTE: THEY WILL TAKE MY ISLAND
Exhibition of new work, May 7 – 30, 2009

The intentional obscuring of forms and melding of colours is the most obvious contributor to the 
aesthetic resonance of George Vergette’s paintings. This deliberate obfuscation is also more 
what the work is about than the words cryptically inlaid beneath and on top of his many layers of
pigmented resin. In this exhibition, Vergette lingers over his fascination with the written word, but, 
unlike in earlier paintings, the relevance of the word as a signifier is greatly decreased, and its 
relevance as a symbol remains as enigmatic as ever. 

In earlier paintings, clear and prominent words featured heavily as compositional elements. Their
meaning was clear, as they were often only one word, but their pertinence to the overall meaning 
of the artwork was open to interpretation. In this exhibition, letters occupy the same theoretical 
territory as marks and colours. They are symbols enacted for the purpose of furthering the visual
language. A shape veiled behind layers of resin, like a stick at the bottom of a shallow lake, is
just as important as the colour emerging onto the surface, or the words, inscribed both 
haphazardly and very deliberately on the placid surface of a painting. These factors work with 
each other to test the tensile strength of what is above and what is below, rendering the heavily
built panels on which the resin sits as delicate and precariously balanced works of art.

Perhaps the best example of the fallibility of the written word as a communication medium is in 
the piece titled “No Use For Irony”. Why, if language is just one imperfect avenue to 
understanding, would we have any use for words used to express meaning that is actually the 
opposite of what is being said? These words, laid out expressively on a black pool of resin 60 
inches square, are a visual statement, felt viscerally when viewing the work and the immediacy of
this experience is unrelated to the meaning of the words. As his artistic practice evolves, 
Vergette is not only evincing the subtleties of the relationship between the written word and the
painted form, but also the potential of paint, pigment and resin to communicate clearly and 
purely.
