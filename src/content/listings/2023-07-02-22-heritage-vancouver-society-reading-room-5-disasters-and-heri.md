In metro Vancouver, we face long-term threats, like coastal flooding from sea level rise,
as well as more frequent extreme weather events. One hazard, though, is unique
because its magnitude and timing are so unpredictable " a major earthquake. While
we are lucky earthquakes are infrequent, we know from Indigenous histories and
geological analyses that major earthquakes are a part of the long-term story of this
region. It is only a matter of time until a damaging earthquake strikes Vancouver. In the
face of such a threat to peoples health, lives, and belongings, what does heritage
matter?

Part 1 in March and April
dove into a speculative future to ask: if a major earthquake
strikes Vancouver, what is left of its heritage? This upcoming Part 2 takes a multi-
generational view of heritage and disasters to ask: what is the meaning of disasters for
future generations? How does a disaster become connected to ones heritage?

Readings and online exhibitions discuss the ways that disasters are remembered
through architecture, monuments, traditions, and cultural practices. We draw examples
from the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, the Great Vancouver Fire of 1886, or the
1917 Halifax explosion, to thousands of years of earthquake knowledge reflected in the
heritage of the Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest.

Answering these questions together will give us the opportunity to explore how
heritage can grow from disaster, and how the lessons of disaster heritage in the
present can help us prepare for the future. Through short readings, videos, and virtual
tours, we will discover some ways in which
the special places and practices that
people value can serve as important and unexpected sources of disaster knowledge.
What lessons can these examples hold for Vancouver, as we face the potential for
future disasters?

This activity is supported by the University of British Columbia's Public Scholars
Initiative. UBC PhD candidate Jonathan Eaton will serve as one of the guest
discussion leaders for this event. Jonathan is an anthropologist whose research
focuses on the relationships between people, heritage, and place when it comes to
disaster planning in Vancouver.
