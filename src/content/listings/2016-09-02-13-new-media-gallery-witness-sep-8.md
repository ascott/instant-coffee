WITNESS

September 8 - November 6
Opening September 8, 7-9 pm

Rafael Lozano-Hemmer (Canada)
Bjrn Schlke (Germany)
France Cadet (France)
Stanza (UK)
Adam Basanta (Canada)

"I can feel myself under the gaze of someone whose eyes I do not 
see, not even discern. All that is necessary is for something to 
signify to me that there may be others there." 
Lacan 

Please join us for a public reception and glass of wine as New 
Media Gallery proudly celebrates its second anniversary & tenth 
exhibition. WITNESS presents five artists who explore the seeing 
machine.

The first written use of the word witness comes from the early 
medieval period. Originally the word meant knowledge, in the sense 
that you could see, observe or know by personal presence. Over 
time the word became understood as a means of establishing 
identity and thus the notion of the eye-witness.

WITNESS explores the notion of a seeing machine and the world it 
sees. The five works convey rigorous control, interconnectivity, 
ubiquitous screens and familiar technology. Yet the power to 
invoke interpretation lies in the indeterminacy of each gaze. 
Throughout the gallery we find various iterations of surveillance and 
vision technologies with the potential to reconfigure public space, 
our habits and the technologies themselves. Surveillance here 
becomes a watchful yet uncertain tool ; a process and way of 
knowing the world (mediated through recording, checking, tracking, 
being watched, suspicion, evasion). 

Do robotic cats dream of electric fish? The hacked robotic cat by 
France Cadet watches a little fish on a big screen: the lovable and 
commodified Nemo. This act of watching becomes a critique 
on...the act of watching. The work references Philip K. DIck's 1968, 
sci-fi novel, 'Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep' in which 
empathy places a significant role. The cat will never capture what 
it sees and desires...perhaps that is the point.

Two i-phones face each other on angled selfie sticks. A familiar 
dial-up tone plays and the Apple Video Chat Platform Face Time 
kicks in. A human face appears on one screen. Another dial tone ; 
another face. This mutual gaze occurs across an unfathomable and 
intimate distance, now a performance space. A truly magical 
Moment by Adam Basanta creates an absurd yet oddly familiar 
situation using familiar telecommunication tools. The technology 
completes a few dizzying optical circuits. We witness the gaze. It 
is a recursive performance: seeing and being seen, voyeurism & 
exhibitionism, many times over. 

The eye opens as you enter the room; follows your every move with 
what could be Orwellian intent. Surface Tension by Rafael Lozano-
Hemmer will probably elicit some level of anxiety in the visitor, 
trapped under its irresistible gaze. The artist has acknowledged the 
predatory, watchful movements of this eye, at the same time 
revealing the horrible truth; it does not see you, it does not care. It 
looks like a biologic eye: it behaves like a machine. It sees: it is 
blind. The work is a paradox. Created at the time of the first Gulf 
War the work references the use of surveillance and drone 
technologies and the role and accuracies of a militaristic gaze.

Vision Machine #3 is a beautiful nightmare. This delicate, 
arthropodic work by Bjrn Schlke is characterized by its "lively 
interior consciousness, it's complex cycles of communication and 
movement and the appearance of irrational character traits and 
distinctive emotional features." With the precision of an arthropod 
its reflective vision relies on various combinations of compound 
eyes that swivel to detect their prey. It is a suspicious, vigilant little 
creature. It sees you. It wants to meet you. Be warned.

A sprawling collection of daisy-chained monitors, watchful orbs and 
speakers give voice to circulating, machinic narratives. The Agency 
at the End of Civilization, by British artist Stanza, presents a parallel 
future-present that combines real-time data with false narratives. In 
this world we are under constant surveillance; we are watched in 
precise detail, our movements are interpreted by machines. Yet the 
interpretation of what we are seeing and hearing becomes 
increasingly uncertain. The work links real video and information 
from hundreds of CCTV cameras in the south of England . Aligned 
to this are millions of car number plates from the UK car number 
plate recognition system (The Internet of Cars Project). Using 
predictive software the machine collects what it is seeing in real 
time, then begins to insert false narratives to create its own version 
of reality. The work speaks to our control of public space and our 
trust in technology.
