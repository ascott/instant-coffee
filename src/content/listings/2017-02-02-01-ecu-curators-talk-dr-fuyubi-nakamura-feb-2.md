Thursday, February 2, 4:30-6:30 pm

Curator's Talk: Dr. Fuyubi Nakamura
Emily Carr University of Art + Design, South Building 301

Dr. Fuyubi Nakamura is a socio-cultural anthropologist. Fuyubi joined the 
Museum of Anthropology at UBC as its Curator for Asia in April 2014. She is also 
an Associate Member of the Departments of Anthropology and Asian Studies 
and is affiliated with the Centre for Japanese Research at UBC. Fuyubi obtained 
a doctorate in socio-cultural anthropology from the University of Oxford (2006) 
after completing an MSc with Distinction in Ethnology and Museum Ethnography 
(1999) also at Oxford. She has taught at the University of Oxford, the Australian 
National University and University of Tokyo, and currently contributes teaching to 
various departments at UBC. She was a guest curator at the National Museum of 
Oriental Art in Buenos Aires (2010-11) and took a leave from academia/museum 
following the 3.11 disaster in Japan, and was involved in relief/recovery activities 
in Miyagi in 2011-2012. She was also the producer for Tokunoshima island art 
project in Japan in 2013-2014. Outside academia, Fuyubi has worked as a 
project coordinator for film festivals organized by NHK (Japan's national 
broadcasting organization) and also in business in Tokyo between her degrees. 
Her publications include Asia through Art and Anthropology: Cultural Translation 
Across Borders (edited with Morgan Perkins and Olivier Krischer, 2013; the joint 
winner of the Best Anthology Prize for 2014 by Art Association of Australia & 
New Zealand) and "Memory in the debris: The 3/11 Great East Japan 
earthquake and tsunami" in Anthropology Today (2012).

Dr. Nakamura talk will speak to the expectation for contemporary art to be 
international and new. With this, increasing numbers of works and artists are 
introduced and circulated far from their cultural origins. Artists of non-Western 
origins in particular are often expected to represent or at least reference the 
cultures of their birthplace to be recognized in the global art world. This process 
of discovery is disconcertingly similar to the work of an earlier generation of 
anthropologists, as well as to the identification of a readymade avant-garde by 
modern artists in the aesthetics of "primitive" art. While most corners of the globe 
have become more physically and digitally accessible, in many cases the self-
appointed cultural centers still see only the otherness that is closest at hand
foreign artists studying in New York or London, or featured in a major biennale, 
for example, who will be enlisted to contribute to the narrative of an ostensibly 
international art space. The fact that such international spaces remain 
overwhelmingly Anglophone should raise questions as to the real implications of 
non-English-speaking artists moving through them. The growing role of artist-
curators, as well as the rise of indigenous curators, as examples of some who 
can have an intimate knowledge, and how their work affects our practices needs 
to be considered as well. This lecture asks whether curators today have become 
among the key translators of artistic and cultural products and those who 
produce them.
