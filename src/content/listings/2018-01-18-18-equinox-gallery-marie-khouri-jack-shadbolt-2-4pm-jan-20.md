MARIE KHOURI: BRONZE
January 20 - February 22, 2018

Vancouver-based sculptor Marie Khouri represents the broad multi-cultural and 
diverse demographic of Western Canada. Born in Egypt and raised in Lebanon, the 
outbreak of the Lebanese Civil War forced her family to move to Canada. As an 
adult she moved to Paris where she trained in fine art at Les Ateliers du Louvre, and 
on her return to Vancouver a retrospective of her life took form in her sculpture, 
reflecting a passion for innovation and spontaneity.

Khouris bronze sculptures are formed through an extensive hands-on process that 
employs traditional sculpting techniques alongside contemporary innovations in 
material and building standards. Her works blend and extend metaphors of 
language, form and the body to propose an inextricable link " both political and 
personal " to a life deeply affected by the complex histories of the Middle East, 
rooted in a profound sense of dislocation and a search for a greater sense of place.

JACK SHADBOLT: THE GHOST UNIVERSE
January 20 - February 22, 2018

Shadbolt began teaching in 1938 at the Vancouver School of Art, while furthering his 
own studies at the Art Students League in New York as well as in London and Paris. 
After his term as an unofficial war artist during WW2, he returned to his position at 
the Vancouver School of Art where he became head of the department and exerted 
considerable influence over young painters. In 1956 Shadbolt represented Canada 
at the Venice Biennale, and in 1972 he was made an Officer of the Order of Canada. 
Over the course of his career, Shadbolt produced a vast body of work that helped 
define a regional aesthetic of nature-based abstraction. 

The body of work presented here was made between 1949 and 1959, the period 
after the war when Shadbolt was developing his formal vocabulary as a painter. 
Influenced as much by Pablo Picasso and Arshile Gorky as by Northwest Coast 
masks and the tumultuous landscape, Shadbolt explored the symbolic and 
abstracted nature of images to address the human condition in an age of anxiety. 
These works portray crawling insects, totemic shapes and stick figures contained in 
fields of colliding forces that allude to the traumas of the land produced by resource 
extraction, the uncertainties of war, and the collisions of Indigenous and colonial 
cultures.
