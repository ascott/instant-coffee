The word stlmexw in the Swxw7mesh language has been historically translated
as a noun, human being. However, recent discussions among Elders and
knowledge keepers found this translation to be inaccurate. It is now widely
understood as being human, a verb signals the privilege and responsibility that
comes along with active participation in a community.

Aaron
Nelson-Moodys 30 year-long career encompasses carving, graphic design,
and metalwork. As both an artist and educator, community has been fundamental to
his artistic approach. This exhibition highlights his activism and advocacy work, as
well as the collaborative nature of his working style. While artists of all cultures invite
onlookers to ponder the existence of life on Earth, Nelson-Moodys work is often
conceived with a strong intention to create opportunities for profound
connection and
shared learning. In doing so, Nelson-Moodys artistic approach actively seeks to
create tangible positive change. Using art in the broadest sense of the word"art as
cultural encounter; art as protest; art as process; art as teacher"Nelson-Moody
invites others in reflection and conversation.

About the Artist

Aaron Nelson-Moody is a Squamish carver and jeweller working in the Coast Salish
tradition. His Swxw7mesh name is Tawx'sin Yexwulla, which translates to
"Splashing Eagle," though most know him as "Splash." He also carries the name
Poolxtun from his adopted father Gerry Oleman, which translates as: the spreading
ripples from a splash of water. Nelson-Moody has been woodcarving since 1996,
under the mentorship of celebrated North Shore artist Xwalacktun. He also studied
jewellery engraving at the Native Education College in Vancouver and gained a
mastery of metal repouss techniques with Valentin Yotkov. Nelson-Moodys works
include the Western Red Cedar doors to the
BC/Canada pavilion at the 2006 Winter
Olympic Games in Turin, several large works for Olympic venues for the 2010
Olympics, house boards for the Squamish/Lilwat Cultural Centre, as well as
engraved, smithed, and repouss metal pieces that are found in collections
throughout Vancouver's Lower Mainland.

Nelson-Moody has worked as an educator throughout the Squamish and Vancouver
areas since 1995. For 10 years Nelson-Moody was involved with the Uts Witness
Project arts and environment program. He also teaches several
Indigenous woodcarving classes at Langara Colleges Fine Art program, and is
currently teaching metalworking at Emily Carr University of Art + Design. He has
been on two Tribal Journeys in the Squamish Nations 50-foot carved cedar canoe
and one in Xwalacktuns family canoe, the Pekultun.

Exhibition Description

Improbable Dances
This body of work explores the way humans move through the landscape.

The nature of connection raises the question of how humans interact with nature: the
nature of our connection to it and our imposition upon it. We seek ways to heal
ourselves and reconnect to nature yet in our zealousness we dont always notice the
harmful effects we have on the rest of the creatures that live in the places we choose
to visit and inhabit.

Are we trying
to get too close, encroaching on animals' space for the thrill, disrupting
nature by creating trails and recreating with vehicles? What are the ripple effects for
the animals that are living their lives in those environments?

A Series of 14 Improbable Dances includes drawings extracted from my
sketchbooks and superimposed onto imagined landscapes. "My practice of
sketchbook drawing in the dark during live dance performances started 30 years ago
and continues to this day." The drawings capture the
essence and spontaneity of
movement in loose gestures that can be, surprisingly, quite accurate.

Walking and paddling through a landscape is like a dance for me and a way to
connect to myself and nature. I created imaginary landscapes with textures and
landscapes that I had experienced in past travels and placed the dancer drawings
within them. Imagine travelling through the world with a lighter footprint.

Artist Biography

Joyce Majiskis work delves into the profound connection to place
and the natural
world. Her background as a biologist and wilderness adventure guide in the Yukon
Territory enriches her artistic practice, leading her to explore remote wild spaces in
the North. With a deep fascination for the intricate interdependence between all
living beings, she meticulously observes and collects objects, infusing her artwork
with a profound sense of curiosity.

Majiski's artistic repertoire spans printmaking, drawing, painting, handmade paper,
sculpture, and multimedia
installation work. She collaborates with musicians and
fellow artists to create immersive environments, reflecting her intuitive approach to
art-making. Joyce's diverse interests and experiences have led her to artist
residencies in 11 countries, and she has showcased her solo exhibitions across
Canada, Mexico, the US, and Europe. She is the visionary behind initiatives such as
the Artist in the Park program in Ivvavik National Park and the Art Under Pressure
Giant Steamroller print event,
demonstrating her commitment to fostering artistic
endeavors both locally and internationally. Joyce has called the Yukon home since
1984.
