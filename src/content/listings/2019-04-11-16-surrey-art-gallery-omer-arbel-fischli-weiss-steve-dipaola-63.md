Artist in Conversation & Opening Reception

Surrey Art Gallery
April 13, 2019 | 6:30'9pm

The opening reception of Omer Arbel: Particles for the Built World, the designers first 
solo exhibit at the Surrey Art Gallery, begins with a conversation between Arbel and 
Gallery Curator Jordan Strom at 6:30pm; reception follows at 7:30pm. The evening will 
also celebrate two other exhibits: The Way Things Go by Peter Fischli and David Weiss 
and Pareidolia by Steve DiPaola.

Particles for the Built World focuses on Arbels experiments with concrete over the past 
five years. What if we poured concrete into fabric forms rather than plywood boxes or 
tube columns used in most building construction? What if we used this technology to 
make other objects and spaces? The astonishing results of these material explorations 
are on display in this exhibit. My intention is to develop a way of working with concrete 
that acknowledges its liquid nature and yields expressive form, he says. These new 
methods could have significant practical and sculptural ramifications to the construction 
industry. In addition to a large sculptural installation, visitors will see a multi-channel 
projected film, architectural models, plans, photographs, fabric relief, and sectional 
collages. These objects, images, and sculptures reimagine domestic and urban life 
outside the flat rectilinear visual language of so much modern design.

The Way Things Go presents a chain reaction of ordinary objects set up in a warehouse, 
complete with weights and pulleys, spilling liquids, and pyrotechnics. For anyone who 
enjoys watching dominoes fall or playing the board game Mouse Trap, this film is for 
you. Fischli and Weisss contraption resembles a Rube Goldberg machine that performs 
a simple task in an overcomplicated way. The artists exploit chemical reactions and laws 
of physics to create a narrative filled with tension, shock, and delight.

In our lobby, Steve DiPaolas multi-screen artwork Pareidolia draws you in with an 
uncanny resemblance to the space youre standing in. For this site-specific work, 
DiPaola turned his video camera on the Surrey Arts Centre and people inside, blurring 
the lines between physical space and its inhabitants. DiPaolas software draws attention 
to the ways in which algorithmic technology is shaping the way we see the world and 
our own sense of identity.
