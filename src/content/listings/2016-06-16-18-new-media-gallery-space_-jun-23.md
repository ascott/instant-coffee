The concept of space has always been relative...to the extent that 
bodies, nations and events have a position relative to each other 
and are affected by time. The works in this exhibition make you 
aware of passing time and of a repetition of recorded histories. The 
works are rife with memory; absence, abandonment and loss. 

Four works by five artists. Voyager-One by Kristina Estell traces 
the path of the 'Voyager-One' space probe in real time. Launched 
in 1977, the probe entered extra terrestrial space in 1990. The 
distance of the probe from earth is translated into colour shifts 
experienced through a tiny light in a completely dark room. 
SPACEJUNK by David Bowen, consists of five robots who trace the 
path of the oldest piece of space junk circling overhead. The robots 
move together in real time when they detect a signal, as if in a 
mechanic ballet. Star City, a 4-channel video installation, by Jane & 
Louise Wilson is the earliest work. The Yuri Gagarin Cosmonaut 
Training Centre (or 'closed military townlet #1), is a place where 
cosmonauts have lived and trained since the 1960's. The camera 
pans across abandoned space pads, control rooms...dwelling on 
empty space suits; entering once forbidden territories. The 
implications and results of this historic space are parsed in rich 
detail. Sunlight by 2012 Turner Prize winner Elizabeth Price is a 2-
channel video installation that incorporates thousands of glass plate 
negatives of the sun. Taken on a daily basis between 1875 and 
1945 they are some of the earliest scientific solar images and were 
discovered by the artist at the Rutherford Appleton Laboratory 
during a residency with Dr. Hugh Mortimer, Space Scientist. Her 
work traces and connects the paths and attitudes of both the 
celestial and human body. The figures of women are captured; 
arms raised to shield their eyes as if from a hot solar gaze. 
Aureolin yellow nail varnish drips and a nostalgic soundtrack 
seduce the viewer with an excess of solar power. New 
Westminster Sky Train Station is directly across from the gallery.
