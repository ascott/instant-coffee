The Fall, 2016 VISR Term:

Monday Nights, 7:00 pm at the Or Gallery, 555 Hamilton St.

Nov. 7th - Jerry Zaslove "Why Are We So Afraid of Revolution?"

Reflections apropos of the 500th anniversary of Martin Luther's posting of
the 95 Theses on the door of the Schlosskirche in Wittenberg.

Who is happy celebrating?

In his 1928 essay "Fire Alarm" Walter Benjamin wrote: "The course of
history as represented in the concept of catastrophe has no more claim on
the thinking man's attention than the kaleidoscope in the hands of a child.
With each new twist, everything collapses into a new order. The image is
thoroughly well-grounded. The concept of the rulers has always been the
mirrors by which the image of "order" was established - The kaleidoscope
must be smashed" (Central Park).

The interplay of revolutionary destruction and the idea of redemption that
Dostoevsky portrayed in the anarcho-nihilists of his novel, The Possessed,
cited by Benjamin and others, had travelled within and beyond the epoch of
the 19th century's fear of revolution and swept into the Russian and German
"revolutions" against imperial nation-states continuing class war by other
means.

Reflecting and extending Benjamin's "Critique of Violence" is it possible
to reflect on "Who is it That Fears Revolution" in an age of mass
annihilation, submission to violence, military attrition, political
assassination, demographic violence, reprisals, privation, dislocation,
collectivization, and massacres and more. The choice is between Wallace
Stevens' "The Emperor of Ice Cream" and versions of Luther's "power
protected inwardness"?

Jerry Zaslove received his B.A. from Western Reserve University, now the
Case Western Reserve University in Cleveland, Ohio. He then pursued
postgraduate studies at the University of Washington, where he received his
Ph.D. in Comparative European literature. Zaslove joined Simon Fraser
University as a charter faculty member in 1965 as an instructor within the
English department. He came to SFU because of its promise to support
interdisciplinary studies and to take advantage of the opportunities that
starting at a brand new university afforded. Once Zaslove completed his
doctorate he was promoted to the rank of assistant professor and was an
associate professor by the early seventies.

Zaslove's teaching and research interests remained comparative and
interdisciplinary; he was particularly interested in myth and folktale, and
the comparison between English, German, and Russian literature.
Nineteenth-century literature was also an area of interest for Zaslove. He
was heavily involved in the development of Humanities at SFU, he helped
found the Humanities programme, guide its later developments into the
Institute for Humanities, established in the early eighties, and created
the humanities department in the late nineties. He also served as the
director of the Institute for Humanities for many years. Zaslove became a
professor emeritus at SFU in 2000 but he continues to be actively involved
in the SFU community as mentor for the Graduate Liberal Arts degree
programme in his capacity as the holder of the Simons Chair.
