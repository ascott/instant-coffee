FEBRUARY 25-26, 2011

As Hal Foster has noted in a recent questionnaire on the subject, contemporaneity--while not a new term has at our present moment assumed a relevance as insistent as it is diverse. Encompassing a wide array of concerns including the ambitions and anxieties of the new in historical and neo-avant-gardes, the epistemological status of collected objects, and the ontological status of the historian, contemporaneity lies at the heart of many of the most pressing questions currently preoccupying the humanities. The 30th annual University of British Columbia Art History Visual Art, and Theory Graduate Symposium provides a unique opportunity to address these questions. Offering a cross-disciplinary forum for reflection and discussion, the symposium will investigate not only the specific ways in which contemporaneity has been and is being mobilized by scholars, critics, artists and curators working today, but also how different notions of contemporaneity engage our collective pasts and
anticipate our shared futures.

Although a well-established topic in discussions of 20th and 21st century art production and reception, recent scholarship has reasserted the centrality of contemporaneity to political, aesthetic and curatorial discourse. The emergence of new technologies, markets and voices have been praised by those championing the political potential of the global village in which diverse cultures can engage immediately with one another as contemporaries, as if freed from the bonds of imperial and colonial histories, while others have cautioned us against too hasty an embrace of globalization and its ahistorical present. The demand that contemporary artists produce work that exposes, critiques or rejects that of their predecessors has led to politically and conceptually ambitious undertakings that have occasioned greater institutional self-reflexivity and sensitivity. However, these same obligations have also lead to the dominance of the new as arguably the only criterion of aesthetic and
curatorial judgment and contributed to the dizzyingly rapid application of the prefix post to any and all artistic endeavor.

Discussions of contemporaneity have been no less vigorous in other areas of research. Recently, Medieval and Early Modern scholars have increasingly framed their research and analyses in terms of contemporaneity. Familiar notions of periodization, context and influence have been both challenged and complemented by a renewed interest in anachronism, citation and reform that have nuanced our understanding of image production and reception.
Similarly, the positions of the historian and the collector as individuals for whom history forms a discrete totality that can be objectively inspected and catalogued have once again become the subjects of intense scrutiny by scholars concerned with the more dynamic temporal qualities of the historical testimony, archive and trace.

The organizing committee seeks proposals for papers that address these and related issues from emerging scholars and practitioners from across the humanities and social sciences, as well as proposals for artworks to be displayed in an exhibition to be held in conjunction with the symposium.
