A Short Story
Al Freeman, Chris Hood, Monique Mouton, Adrianne Rubenstein
Saturday, February 27th Saturday, March 26th 
Opening Saturday, February 27th 6:00 10:00PM

Field Contemporary
17 West Broadway, Vancouver

I wanted to get started writing, and my friend suggested I begin with a
short story: a character study with one main event, and an abundance of
shadows (in a short story, absence takes on a presence). There is drama in
a short story, and events are noted with room for a few stray suggestions.
There is a crux, a clang, as one thing bangs against another. In a short
story, there is one distinct mark traced over the earth.

If you write a number of short stories you can work your way up to a big
one, it's like an exercise. Characters can take shape, like seeds newly
planted, the beginning of a life. The short story has momentum; it is also
just long enough without getting you too entangled. It is not The Odyssey.

Another reason for a short story is how perfectly it describes the space
around one of Monique Mouton's breathlessly charged forms. Shhhh... they say,
as they pull you under the water. Ms. Mouton grew up in Colorado and
earned her Bachelor's degree at Emily Carr in Vancouver. She is familiar
with mountains and the wispy things that grow on them. Here in inclement
weather, behind a boulder, is life that seems to exist only by virtue of
the sun's pulling it out of its hiding. Slow erosion, a dominant whisper.
Distinct in color and in softness, her paintings yearn beautifully and
strangely, describing a state of being that is bittersweet until it pops--
and becomes pure and transcendent.

Al Freeman's work declares an identity that is comedic, sincere and
baffling. Her muse, a man named Ortelio, a former flame and concubine, an
everyman who worked in I.T. on Wall Street, wears many costumes and poses
here. You feel for him because he is a sympathetic character, not unlike a
bashful elephant dressed in a tutu. The portrayal is as much self-portrait
as commentary on gender bias in art. Al stands for Allison, and the artist
works with her left hand, though she is predisposed to use her right; both
stances invert conventions instituted during Ms. Freeman's matriculation at
the number one art school in America. These may be clues that Ms. Freeman
does not want you to know, but here they are laid out on the table for the
sake of the short story.

People think they know things, but stories are vastly distorted when they
come in the form of memory rather than document (and even history is
distorted, as we all know). An early work of Chris Hood's when he was still
in art school in San Francisco depicted a sticker from Toy Story-- a flying
pig puffed out like a balloon floating over cream-colored stationery-- with
the words "Fuck Face" emblazoned over the top in red bubble letters. It
conjures up what you think long after you first fall in love and you want
to write the person's name in blood, the moment when instead you say
goodbye... So Long, Fuck Face was an early venture into appropriation for Mr.
Hood, whose endearing works poke at you like acupuncture needles to the heart. 

Adrianne Rubenstein makes paintings whose starting points are oblique
memories of adolescent self-righteousness and fluttering identity. Images
start out with a craving and are rendered ambiguously, hopelessly. Layers
of paint and scuffling brushwork recall the swirling pattern from an old
friend's duvet cover, an earring she coveted from a downtown kiosk in the
90s located on eBay in 2015, or simply a window at night, with a branch.
The images aim to replicate the aches of tweenhood that go unarticulated
until decades later when the smell of an old closet makes it all flood back
into the chest. Together the works excavate the home she holds in her
mind's eye - transformed by dreams and envisioned anew.
