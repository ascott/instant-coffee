Reoccurrence: Serial Motifs | Rebecca Brewer, Chrystal Fisher and Allison
Tweedie
Curated by Emmy Lee and Stephanie Rebick
Presented by the Helen Pitt Gallery at 221A Artist Run Centre, 221 E.
Georgia St., Vancouver BC

November 19 - December 18, 2010
Wednesday - Saturday, noon - 5pm
Opening reception and book launch, Friday, November 19, 8pm

[Images, left to right: Rebecca Brewer - The Peasant, 2010; Chrystal Fisher
- Little Mountain, 2007; Allison Tweedie - Median Lean-To, 2010]

This exhibition brings together three emerging, Vancouver-based artists who
use pattern in their work as an optical device to interrupt their
representational landscapes. These artists use abstracted repetitive forms
to organize the pictorial space, define depth, foreground-background
relations and create movement in their works. Their use of pattern
encourages the viewer to try and form optical associations to organize and
understand the visual field.

Multi-disciplinary artist Rebecca Brewer uses patterns and found imagery
from 1970s nature-based, fashion and educational magazines to create
biomorphic shapes and experiment with colour and form. Brewer layers
incised shapes to build totemic figures; the disparate patterns she employs
creates a dissonant effect for the viewer who must work to decipher an
overall image.

Chrystal Fisher's work features architectural structures that she uses to
layer the visual field and construct focal points for the viewer. In her
more abstract compositions, she uses geodesic forms to frame repetitive
motifs and layers them to create complex spatial relations. In more
representational works, she borrows directly from interior design, using
patterns found on tiling, couches and other domestic objects. While these
shapes act as quasi-architectural structures, they also reference the
history of abstract painting with the exploration of pure form.

Allison Tweedie's abstracted watercolours include figures in landscapes and
leisure scenes; she uses the fluid nature of the medium to create dreamy
scenes in which the people and their surroundings appear to blend together.
Tweedie uses oil pastel to disrupt the otherwise harmonious surface of her
images and introduce patterning which adds complexity in both its texture
and bold palette. The patterning in her work results from layering two
conflicting media that maintain their separate identities.

Although pattern is often relegated to the world of design, these artists
use repetitive motifs to challenge the visual purity of their imagery by
breaking up the picture plane. Pattern is used both as a means to insert
abstraction into representational scene as well as a building block by
which to create figuration. Brewer, Fisher and Tweedie use abstracted
repetitive motifs as a device through which to challenge the traditional
divide between representation and abstraction.

The exhibition is accompanied by a publication, Reoccurrence, containing
colour plates, an essay by the curators and a challenging dialogue between
the artists.

Since its inception as a student-run gallery in 1975, the Helen Pitt
Gallery has been through many changes. Originally named after the Vernon,
B.C. art patron who established a scholarship endowment for young artists,
the gallery survived the loss of the Vancouver School of Art's support in
the early 1980s, when that school changed its name to Emily Carr College of
Art and Design and moved to Granville Island. The Unit 306 Society For The
Democratization Of Art took over the management of the gallery in 1982,
which then became the Unit/Pitt Gallery. A subsequent move to larger
premises on Powell Street in Gastown coincided with another change of name,
this time to the ironically overstuffed Pitt International Galleries (PIG).
Two moves later, in a storefront on the advancing edge of Yaletown
gentrification, the Helen Pitt name was restored. In the fall of 2009,
faced with massive losses of revenue due to disastrous policy decisions
made by the B.C. government, the gallery closed its most recent location on
the fringes of Gastown and currently conducts its programming in whatever
spaces become available.

The Helen Pitt Gallery is financially assisted by the Canada Council, the
