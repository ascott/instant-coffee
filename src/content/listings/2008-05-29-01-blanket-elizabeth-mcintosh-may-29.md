Blanket Contemporary Art: Elizabeth McIntosh: Opening Reception Thursday May
29, 6-9pm

Elizabeth McIntosh
May 29 to June 28, 2008
Opening Reception: Thursday, May 29th 6-9 pm
Artist in Attendance

To many, the handle Geometric Abstraction evokes an ethic of meticulousness and unrelenting rigor. For Vancouver artist Elizabeth McIntosh, this realm behind the name is merely a jumping off point. Using a working vocabulary of varied shapes, she references outside of the limits set by Geometric Abstraction, resisting its autonomy, and creates canvases which shift, disrupt and challenge the expectations of the viewer. Opening on May 29th, Blanket is pleased to present a show of new works by Elizabeth McIntosh. This is the artist¹s second solo show with the gallery.

McIntosh received her BFA from York University in 1992 before going on to earn an MFA from the Chelsea College of Art and Design in London in 1996. Her work has appeared at galleries and institutions including the Vancouver Art Gallery (as part of the hugely successful 2006 group exhibition PAINT); the Dalhousie Art Gallery in Dalhousie Nova Scotia (as part of the Hungry Eyes group exhibition); Clementine Gallery in New York and Perugi Artecontemporanea in Padua, Italy.  The recipient of the 2004 Steam Whistle Arts Award for Best Painting Exhibition, McIntosh recently also had a painting acquired by the National Gallery of Canada. She currently lives and works in Vancouver, where she teaches at the Emily Carr Institute of Art and
Design.

McIntosh¹s new paintings aim to represent different kinds of spaces, yet in a subtler manner and with a more vibrant palette than her older works. In one painting, the bottom left of the picture ground is dominated by a kind of crystal, a curious amalgam of cube and hexagon. The lines, or ³folds² in this mass seem to make no geometric sense; however, the smooth planes
rendered in conflicting and yet complimentary shades of carmine, scarlet, grey, Carolina blue and white create a composite whole which, outlined almost aggressively in black, has a pleasing, sensuous quality. Adding to the mystery of the piece is the fact that the background (a thin wash of saffron yellow) barely conceals more geometric forms beneath the surface. Most notable of these is a kind of squared ³pipe² which seems to feed directly into the crystal, animating its ever-morphing planes and folds with breath.

In another piece, we are presented with the makings of a geometrically informed painting in predominantly cooler tones (lavender, grey, pear, powder blue) that seems to feature very few perfect triangles. Most seem to have rounded or imperfect edges, or are in conflict with irregular (non-triangular) polygons, some also with swooping, bulbous edges. The
perimeters of these shapes are loosely joined, allowing us to catch glimpses of peeping filaments of white, and the whole ground is contained by a cheerfully uneven border in forest green. The ³glitches² in the work do nothing to detract from its strength or dynamism; indeed, there is the impression that new forms are emerging as we look, evoking our own surprise but also conveying a sense of astonishment behind the hand that made it.

McIntosh herself has stated that her aim is to build complex paintings that change as the viewer looks at them, that allow themselves to be discovered. In this way, there is a direct correlation between the juxtapositions of spatial and technical elements and that of intent and outcome. What is constant is that there are no rules; we are invited to come along for the ride and witness first hand the experimental aspects and emotional vicissitudes of a practice that is as playful as it is rigorous.
