Vancouver Art Gallery | Curatorial Talk: Grant Arnold on Gareth Moore: 
Allochthonous Window | SEPT 17, 7pm

Vancouver Art Gallery
Curatorial Talk: Grant Arnold on Gareth Moore: Allochthonous Window
Tuesday September 17, 7pm
In the Gallery, 4th Floor

Curator Grant Arnold will speak about the exhibition of work by Gareth Moore 
and Emily Carr, entitled In Dialogue With Carr, Gareth Moore: Allochthonous 
Window.

About the exhibition:

Emily Carr's travels to remote locations and the ceramic objects she 
produced for the tourist market serve as the framework for the Vancouver-
based artist Gareth Moore's new work, Allochthonous Window. The term 
"allochthonous window" is taken from structural geology. An allochthon is a 
large rock deposit that has been displaced from the location in which it was 
originally formed. A hole in the deposit that makes visible the geological 
forces that shifted the allochthon from one place to another is referred to as 
an allochthonous window. This term evokes the processes of displacement 
that have brought the objects in this exhibition together and the frameworks, 
privileges and histories-both geological and social-that shape a viewer's 
encounter with the work at this specific place and time.

For In Dialogue with Carr: Gareth Moore, Moore travelled through Europe, 
North America and Asia to investigate sites in which stone has a notable 
significance, including some which date to prehistoric eras and others 
inhabited more recently. During his travels-to such places as the Rock 
Shelters of Bhimbetka in India, the Geumneung Stone Park in South Korea, 
the Gambassi Grotto of Maurizio Becherini in Italy and Sproat Lake in British 
Columbia-Moore produced stone carvings that he exchanged or sold to fellow 
travellers, as well as plein-air drawings made with substances from the sites 
he visited which now serve as documents of his journey. Stone and the 
multiple forms in which it appears here-the cement pathway, the concrete 
figure and the grey limestone whitewash that covers the base on which 
objects are arranged-links the exhibition space to the locations where Moore's 
materials were sourced.

Moore has also used materials collected during his travels to make wall 
drawings that allude to the specific physical structure of the Vancouver Art 
Gallery and its transformation from a courthouse to an art museum. For 
example, the grid of drawings in the adjacent room corresponds precisely to 
panes of glass in the building's exterior windows which were covered by walls 
built to hang artwork when the site was repurposed in 1983. Questions of 
access to space and a site's function are brought into play through Moore's 
positioning of elements of the work on the adjacent roof just outside the 
interior gallery, an area that visitors can enter only during specifically 
scheduled times.

Moore's practice focuses on processes of transformation and exchange that 
are made evident through arrangements of found and constructed objects. 
While these objects are unremarkable on their own, they take on meaning as 
they are positioned within a larger configuration that forms the completed 
artwork. Moore's work often involves periods of extensive travel and emerges 
from his encounters-both planned and accidental-with fellow travellers, 
residents and locales. He often seeks out unconventional sites in which to 
exhibit his work. Past projects have included using found materials to 
construct a rustic dwelling in a park in Kassel, Germany and the temporary 
reconfiguration of a vacant grocery store into a combination 
gallery/confectionary in a residential neighbourhood on the east side of 
Vancouver. In this respect, Moore's method of producing work speaks to a 
desire to bridge the gap between art and daily life, and to engage audiences 
and situations outside the traditional reach of the art world.

The use of Carr's ceramics as a touchstone draws Moore's work into a 
complex and sometimes vexed array of historical moments. Carr began to 
make ceramic objects-flower jars, pots, plates and miniature totems―in 
1924. At the time, Carr was disheartened by the lack of an audience or 
support for her art. She made very few paintings and turned to the production 
of items that could be marketed commercially as a more viable way to 
augment her meagre income. Catering to the tourist market, Carr decorated 
hundreds of ceramic objects with motifs taken from cultures of the indigenous 
peoples of the Canadian West Coast, a practice that has been widely 
criticized over the past twenty-five years. Carr was herself was uneasy with 
the placement of stylized ravens and frogs on lamp bases, bowls and 
ashtrays which were then sold in Vancouver, Banff, Jasper, Calgary and 
Ottawa. As she noted in her book Growing Pains, "I hated myself for 
prostituting Indian Art: our Indians did not 'pot,' their designs were not 
intended to ornament clay."
As Carr saw her ceramics as a commercial enterprise they are seen to hold 
less aesthetic significance than her paintings. In choosing to present his work 
in dialogue with this "subordinate" component of Carr's oeuvre, Moore directs 
our attention to the histories and narratives that are carried in objects or 
gestures that are often overlooked and invites us to reflect on broader 
questions concerning the intersection of aesthetic value, cultural appropriation 
and economic necessity. In Dialogue with Carr: Gareth Moore features a new 
work by Moore that includes objects and materials gathered during his 
journeys, as well as a selection of Emily Carr's ceramics, presented in 
relation to the specific physical structure of the gallery space.
