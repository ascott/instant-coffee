In her artistic practice, Amy Lam produces models of exchange that involve activism,
community building and the sustained development of networks that address difficult
questions. Her work often invokes humour to support constructive discourses that
challenge imperialist systems and connect complex, often underrecognized, narratives to
a broader public. With Make-Believe Bathroom, Lam creates a virtual space that provides
a moment of escape from the all-consuming pressures of day to day life, acknowledging
the ways
that retreat is often undervalued as an activity that enables personal and political
survival.

Make-Believe Bathroom recognizes that, within institutions, bathrooms are oftentimes
places of escape. Inspired by her own experiences in academia and at various
workplaces, Lam addresses the need for seclusion and how this enables other kinds of
relation to unfold. Make-Believe Bathroom is a website that functions as an idealized place
where one can go for a short break during online classes or lectures, work hours, or family
gatherings on Zoom. In this space of respite, visitors are invited to check their phone,
gaze out a cobwebbed window, talk to a stranger, or perhaps cry. Productivity is on pause
while the serendipity and randomness of anonymous encounters unfold. Unlike many
online spaces, which are designed to surveil or market to us, Make-Believe Bathroom
provides a possible reprieve, a place to reflect or to simply do nothing.

The spaces of public bathrooms have been historically
contested, with class, gender, race,
and ability determining who gets access to what. The shut-downs of the pandemic have
made it especially difficult to find dignified public places with privacy, where one can take
care of one's intimate needs. In this time of crisis and change, Make-Believe Bathroom
presents a space for possible community that is simultaneously non-functional, a public
toilet that both misses and exceeds its purpose.

Amy Lam is an artist and writer. She was born in Hong Kong and currently lives in Toronto.
Lam has worked with Jon McCurley as the artist duo Life of a Craphead. Their work
spanned conceptual art, performance, media art, and curating. Lam has also collaborated
with Aliya Pabani, Onyeka Igwe, Margaux Williamson, and Sheila Heti on performance,
film and writing projects, and has participated in residencies at Macdowell, the Delfina
Foundation and the Banff Centre. Lam is a founding member of Friends of Chinatown
Toronto, a grassroots group working
against displacement in Torontos downtown
Chinatown.

Jenn Jackson is a curator, writer and researcher. She has held positions at a range of
public and private organizations. Jackson has written on contemporary art for catalogues,
books and journals and is co-editor of Haunt. She is a guest co-curator, with artists
Jeneen Frei Njootli and Christian Vistan, of the next civic triennial at the Vancouver Art
Gallery.
